# Illustrated food preparation and choking prevention

Food readiness varies. Match texture, shape, and size to the child's current chewing and swallowing skills, seat the child upright, and watch continuously during meals. The diagram is a teaching aid, not a guarantee that a food is safe.

![Examples of safer preparation and foods to avoid](../assets/food-preparation-guide.svg)

## Prepare common foods differently

- Cut grapes and cherry tomatoes into quarters rather than serving them whole and round.
- Cut hot dogs lengthwise into quarters before slicing; processed meats are also high in sodium, so limit them.
- Cook hard vegetables and fruits until soft, or prepare them in developmentally appropriate small pieces.
- Spread nut or seed butter thinly. Never serve a thick spoonful or sticky chunk.
- Remove pits, seeds, bones, tough skin, and large chunks.
- Offer foods soft enough to mash with a fork when the child's chewing skill requires it.

These examples follow [CDC choking prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/infant-toddler-nutrition/foods-and-drinks/choking-hazards.html) and [AAP choking guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/injuries-emergencies/Pages/Choking-Prevention.aspx). The AAP advises pieces no larger than one-half inch for infants and young children, while preparation still needs to match the individual child. The drawing is not printed to scale; do not use it as a measuring template.

## Avoid common high-risk foods

Whole nuts and seeds, popcorn, gum, hard or sticky candy, marshmallows, whole grapes or cherry tomatoes, raw hard vegetable pieces, large meat or cheese chunks, and thick chunks of nut butter can be choking hazards. This list is not exhaustive.

## Mealtime safety

- Seat the child upright in a high chair or another safe eating place.
- Do not let the child eat while walking, crawling, riding in a stroller, or traveling in a car.
- Keep meals calm and avoid rushing or distracting the child.
- An attentive adult stays close and watches throughout the meal.
- Ask the child's clinician about choking first aid and consider an accredited infant/child first-aid course.

## From 12 months

At around 12 months, many children can practice finger feeding, supervised spoon use, and drinking from an open cup held by an adult. Breastfeeding can continue as desired. CDC says families may transition from infant formula at 12 months to plain, pasteurized whole cow's milk or a suitable fortified unsweetened dairy alternative; individual nutrition, allergy, and growth needs belong with the child's clinician. Toddler drinks are generally unnecessary. See [CDC cup skills](https://www.cdc.gov/infant-toddler-nutrition/mealtime/fingers-spoons-forks-and-cups.html) and [CDC formula transition guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/infant-toddler-nutrition/formula-feeding/how-much-and-how-often.html).

Do not use this guide to diagnose swallowing problems. Coughing, gagging, difficulty managing textures, recurrent chest illness, or other feeding concerns should be discussed promptly with a clinician.
