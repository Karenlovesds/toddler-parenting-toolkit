# Development: observe, support, and ask early

CDC checkpoints describe skills at least 75% of children can do by a given age. They support monitoring and discussion; they are not diagnostic tests or a score of intelligence. The selections below are brief examples, not the complete checklists. Open the linked CDC checklist at each checkpoint.

## At 15 months

Examples include attempting one or two words beyond parental names, requesting help by pointing, a few independent steps, and stacking two objects. [Full CDC 15-month checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/15-months.html)

## At 18 months

Examples include attempting at least three words beyond parental names, following a single spoken instruction without a gesture, walking independently, and making scribbles. [Full CDC 18-month checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html)

## At 24 months

Examples include combining at least two words, running, kicking a ball, and eating with a spoon. [Full CDC 2-year checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html)

## Between checkpoints

Write a natural example when you notice something new: date, setting, what he did, and whether you helped. Use “seen,” “emerging,” or “not yet observed”; an unobserved skill is not automatically absent. Ask daycare what they see, too. Avoid daily testing or pressuring him to demonstrate.

There are no separate CDC checklists for 16, 17, or 19–23 months. Those repository pages offer flexible play themes without assigning new milestone deadlines. Discuss missing milestones, skill loss, or any concern promptly with his clinician. A reassuring activity log cannot rule out a developmental difficulty.

If he was born prematurely or has a medical/developmental condition, ask his clinician how to interpret age and adapt activities. Use the [observation template](../templates/milestone-observation.md) to prepare for that discussion.
